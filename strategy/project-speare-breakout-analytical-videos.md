# Project Speare — Breakout Analytical Video Protocol

Updated: 16 October 2026
Applies to Cube / The Cube Analyst ideation, research, structure, writing, packaging and review. A supplement to the existing Project Speare guidance, not a replacement for its fact-checking, structure or creator-approved voice rules.

## Why this update exists: an observed breakout, not a proven formula

The 13:38 upload "WoW Forever Has the Same Problem as Old School RuneScape" had about 195K public views in the owner's October 2 channel snapshot. "The Grand Exchange Was Never Built for This" showed about 52K and "OSRS Is Slowly Moving Away From Polling" about 26K, at different upload ages. An earlier 46:16 OSRS-versus-Classic-WoW essay had about 8.8K in an older snapshot. These descriptive facts cannot isolate causes: no matched-age impressions, CTR, traffic sources, engaged-view counts or retention curves for the breakout were supplied. Cross-game comparisons, length, and WoW coverage alone are demonstrably insufficient as explanations.

Plausible factors to test, not requirements to impose:
- A major live announcement created demand for a practical interpretation.
- The premise mattered to both WoW and OSRS audiences without asking either to know the channel.
- The comparison was explanatory: OSRS's history supplied a real precedent, not just a second game logo.
- The video asked a hard specific design question: how can an older MMO keep developing without making its existing content irrelevant?
- The middle used concrete mechanisms and records: reward space, situational gear, Bandos/Torva-style item preservation, vertical raid tiers, and polling/governance.
- The argument moved from announcement and comparison to design mechanism, longer-term difficulty, and trust.
- The package made an immediate, clear, consequential promise.

A comparable video may be lore, history, straight news, a positive explanation, or one-game design analysis. Never force a topical crisis, a contrarian opinion, a WoW/OSRS comparison or a particular chapter count.

## Stage 1 — Ideation: screen for an audience and an actual story

Do this **before** substantial research or scriptwriting.

Record:
1. **Who cares?** Name the actual potential viewer groups and the reason the subject matters to them. For topical ideas, use dated evidence of interest (recent questions, comparable videos, searches, active disputes). An announcement is evidence that news occurred; it is not evidence of audience size.
2. **Why now?** What changed, and how useful will the video remain on its realistic publication date? Evergreen history need not invent an immediate news angle.
3. **What is the central unanswered question?** Prefer a consequential mechanic, design constraint, decision, mystery, or overlooked history over an encyclopedia of features.
4. **What does Cube add?** A grounded cross-game precedent, hidden mechanism, precise history, source conflict, rigorous example, or distinctive interpretation. A useful direct explanation can win without an artificial spin.
5. **Can the idea carry a video?** Write three different potential developments, not three paraphrases of the thesis; identify a credible counterexample or complication. If nothing substantial remains after the premise, shorten, reframe or drop the idea.
6. **Can a stranger understand the promise?** Draft at least two title/thumbnail directions and check they accurately describe what the evidence will deliver.

Use a **subjective editorial evaluation** across audience demand, broad relevance, unique contribution, depth/evidence, narrative development and packaging clarity. Optional 1–5 ratings can help compare pitches, but they are not calibrated probabilities or view predictions. Truthfulness and adequate substance remain hard gates. No arbitrary numerical score can rescue a weak premise.

Prioritize interesting connections when they actually explain something: one game's current decision illuminated by another game's earlier implementation; two communities facing different versions of the same incentive; a historical choice revealed by a new change. Reject a comparison included solely to widen keywords.

## Stage 2 — Research: work out what really explains the issue

Preserve existing Speare source hierarchy and claim ledger: **question → primary evidence → reasoning → supported conclusion → conditions or counterevidence**.

For a major topical design essay, deliberately examine:
- The exact event, date, implementation and version; distinguish announcements, beta plans and live rules.
- The mechanic or constraint and what it changes for different players.
- Concrete historical/cross-game examples, including significant differences that make the analogy imperfect.
- Long-term consequences, limits, possible adaptation and observed outcomes. Never promote incentive logic into claims about typical player behavior without evidence.
- Strong competing interpretations or cases that could change the argument.
- The questions players are actively asking, without treating Reddit as a representative poll or as a primary source for game implementation.

Research broadly, narrate selectively. A fact earns time when it establishes the problem, explains a causal mechanism, changes the viewer's understanding, tests the conclusion, or illustrates a meaningful consequence.

**Thin-idea kill switch:** if the core observation can be explained in a minute or two and research produces only adjacent facts or repeated restatements, make a short factual piece or scrap the long-form video. The rejected 13 October campfire pitch is a warning against expanding a modest observation into a shallow multi-chapter script.

## Stage 3 — Structure: escalate the understanding, not the section count

A *possible* structure for timely comparative design analysis:
1. The recognizable event and its central question.
2. The unexpected but relevant historical or cross-game precedent.
3. The concrete mechanism: rules, incentives, examples, exact numbers where verified, real consequences.
4. The hard limitation: where the precedent may fail or where the proposed solution produces a new problem.
5. The meaningful endpoint: supported judgment, historical outcome, remaining design choice or governance question.

This is an example, not a mandatory template. Chronological documentaries, lore investigations, technical explanations, rankings and news reporting can have different structures.

For **every proposed chapter**, record:
- What the viewer understands before this section.
- The genuinely new evidence, decision, mechanism or objection here.
- Precisely what changes in their understanding.
- Why the following section is now necessary or useful.

The strongest middle section should do the hardest explanatory work. Avoid starting with long basics known to the game's audience. Unequal chapter lengths are appropriate. If two chapters add the same understanding, merge them; if the ending only repeats the introduction, finish earlier. A chapter with a new topic but no new explanatory contribution is not progress.

**Escalation audit:** the argument should become more specific, more consequential or more accurately qualified as the video proceeds; it need not become more dramatic. Strong objections must be allowed to change the conclusion, not dismissed theatrically. Do not manufacture loops that hide the premise from the viewer.

Runtime follows substance: the 13:38 breakout does not establish the optimal video length. A focused 10–18-minute treatment is a useful candidate, while 20–30+ minutes are justified only by distinct necessary sections, reporting or historical developments. Do not pad 12 minutes of reasoning into 30.

## Stage 4 — Title, thumbnail and hook

The title should connect a recognizable thing to a specific reason to watch: a meaningful implication, question, problem, revealing history or supported claim. The thumbnail adds recognition or a visual contrast, not a second full sentence. "We NEED To Talk About X" is a weak default when the actual consequence can be named.

Package for a stranger; speak to an informed viewer. Test whether a viewer from each intended audience can tell what they will learn. Don't make claims of disaster, copying, fraud, hidden motives, firstness or universal outrage without evidence.

The first 15–30 seconds should identify the specific object, central question and why the answer is non-obvious; begin satisfying the title rather than promising everything for later. Keep intro/history proportionate to what viewers need.

## Stage 5 — Script: depth without padding

Retain Project Speare's approved spoken tone and writing calibration: direct conversational English; specific game nouns and concrete player decisions; varied sentence rhythms; concise useful transitions; no repeated explanation; selective personal presence only when authentic; no invented anecdotes. Continue avoiding the word "distinction", automatic "not X, it's Y" constructions, defensive disclaimers and formal filler.

Develop the main mechanism in enough detail that knowledgeable viewers learn something they did not already know. Use examples to prove or test the argument, not to repeat it. Prefer meaningful, decisive evidence over a long feature list. Let a strong point land and move on. Finish after the actual answer, optionally with a short natural close; do not mechanically recap.

## Stage 6 — Learn from results (48h and 7d)

Compare future releases at the same age and, when possible, within Browse, Suggested and Search separately.

- **Reach:** impressions and audience mix; did the potential audience expand?
- **Conversion:** CTR by traffic source and engaged views, with their definition and relevant denominators; did people meaningfully choose the video?
- **Viewing:** first-30s retention, major dips/spikes, AVD and watch time; did the opening deliver the promise and the middle earn attention?
- **Return/resonance:** new/returning viewers, subscriber conversion and the substance of comments; did people value the analysis?

Do not use public views and engaged views interchangeably, or call their difference mid-video abandonment. Public views, engaged views, CTR, AVD and retention describe different stages. A retention spike may mean replay or confusion, not automatically delight. No single successful video establishes a causal theory; update the working hypotheses when repeated comparable uploads supply evidence.

## Standard outputs to carry forward

**Ideation:** audience and dated interest evidence; one-sentence promise; central question; original contribution; two package candidates; depth/feasibility gate.

**Research:** source-backed mechanics/history, competing interpretations, analogy limits, claim confidence, and selected useful findings.

**Structure:** purposeful connected chapters, exact change in understanding for each, strongest middle explanation, cuts and thin-premise audit.

**Script:** faithfully packaged hook; lean setup; clear mechanisms; rising explanatory stakes; natural creator-approved voice; earned ending.

**Postmortem:** matched-age metrics by traffic source where available; observed strengths/weaknesses; alternative causes; editorial change to test next.

**North star:** Find questions that players already have, or will immediately recognize, and use game knowledge and research to offer an answer that routine coverage cannot supply. Keep histories and lore that stand on their own.
