# OSRS-Adjacent Pivot — Top 3 Ideas

These are the strongest current bridge topics for slowly moving The Cube Analyst from pure Old School RuneScape essays toward broader MMO and game-design analysis without abruptly abandoning the existing audience.

## 1. Can You Actually Own Something in a Video Game?

**Pivot risk:** Very low  
**Potential:** 10/10 as a transition video

### Core angle
Start with the Old School RuneScape Mod Jed case: a RuneScape developer steals billions of in-game gold, sells it for real money, and creates the legal question of what exactly was stolen.

That lets the video begin squarely inside OSRS, but then naturally broaden into a much larger question: can a player actually own a virtual item when the game, servers and account all belong to someone else?

### Causal spine
A RuneScape developer takes in-game wealth.

**But** the wealth exists only inside Jagex's game.

**Therefore** calling it theft requires deciding whether virtual gold can count as property.

**But** players routinely spend real money, real time and sometimes thousands of hours acquiring virtual assets.

**Therefore** the question extends far beyond RuneScape: CS skins, MMO gold, Steam inventories, Ultimate Team items, purchased digital games, accounts and live-service shutdowns all sit inside the same ownership problem.

### Why it works for The Cube Analyst
- Strong OSRS opening for the existing audience.
- The actual subject is general gaming.
- Has legal, economic and game-design dimensions.
- Clear investigative question with a real answer to discover.
- Easy bridge into later videos about preservation, virtual economies and digital purchases.

### Packaging
**Primary title:** Can You Actually Own Something in a Video Game?

**Thumbnail concept:** OSRS coin stack / valuable item with a large `PROPERTY?` label.

### Expansion points
- Mod Jed / OSRS gold case
- What a game EULA says players own
- Virtual goods versus licences
- Real-money secondary markets
- CS / Steam inventories
- MMO accounts and gold
- What happens to "owned" items when servers shut down
- Whether time investment changes the intuitive meaning of ownership

## 2. Why Are Old Games Winning Again?

**Pivot risk:** Very low  
**Potential:** Very high broader-audience ceiling

### Core angle
Old School RuneScape is not an isolated nostalgia anomaly. Players across gaming increasingly spend time with games that are ten, fifteen or twenty years old, while publishers keep reviving, remastering and maintaining games that should theoretically have been made obsolete by newer technology.

The interesting question is not simply why people feel nostalgic. It is why technological progress no longer reliably makes old games worse than new ones.

### Causal spine
Videogames used to age rapidly because new hardware and design advances made older games look and feel obsolete.

**But** many of today's most played and culturally durable games are old.

**Therefore** technical progress is no longer enough to replace them.

**But** newer games usually offer more content, better graphics and more modern systems.

**Therefore** something else must have become more valuable: accumulated content, familiarity, social history, low hardware requirements, strong identities, mod ecosystems, persistent progress, community knowledge and distrust of modern monetisation.

### Why it works for The Cube Analyst
- OSRS can remain the main case study while the thesis becomes much broader.
- Natural overlap with WoW Classic, Minecraft, Guild Wars, Counter-Strike and retro revivals.
- Large addressable audience beyond RuneScape.
- Evergreen even if prompted by current nostalgia / revival trends.
- Fits the channel's analytical style better than a generic nostalgia retrospective.

### Packaging
**Primary title:** Why Are We All Playing Old Games Again?

**Alternative:** Old Games Are Winning

### Expansion points
- OSRS as an extreme case
- WoW Classic and parallel legacy ecosystems
- Minecraft nostalgia / older versions
- Persistent progression and sunk social history
- Content density accumulated over many years
- Mods and community infrastructure
- Hardware accessibility
- Modern monetisation fatigue
- Why graphical age matters less for stylised games
- Why old games can now improve instead of simply becoming obsolete

## 3. Why Making an MMO Sequel Is Almost Impossible

**Pivot risk:** Low  
**Potential:** 9.5/10 while Guild Wars 3 is topical

### Core angle
A normal sequel can replace its predecessor. An MMO sequel has to replace not just a game, but years or decades of player progress, possessions, friendships, knowledge and history.

Guild Wars 3 makes the question current, while RuneScape provides one of the best historical case studies of what happens when an MMO tries to move its audience forward.

### Causal spine
Developers eventually want newer technology, better combat, cleaner systems and a fresh starting point.

**Therefore** a sequel seems logical.

**But** MMO players may have thousands of hours invested in the old game.

**Therefore** abandoning the old world imposes a cost that ordinary sequels do not have.

**But** carrying everything forward can recreate the baggage the sequel was supposed to escape.

**Therefore** MMO developers face an almost impossible choice: reset too much and players feel robbed; preserve too much and the new game cannot truly start over.

### Why it works for The Cube Analyst
- RuneScape 2 / RuneScape 3 / OSRS gives existing viewers a familiar route into the subject.
- Guild Wars 3 supplies a current hook.
- WoW provides the opposite strategy: continue expanding one persistent game instead of replacing it.
- The topic explains a much broader industry pattern rather than merely comparing games.
- Strong built-in but/therefore structure.

### Packaging
**Primary title:** Why MMO Sequels Almost Never Work

**Alternative:** Why Making an MMO Sequel Is Almost Impossible

### Expansion points
- RuneScape's unusual evolution into RS3 and OSRS
- Guild Wars → Guild Wars 2 → Guild Wars 3
- Hall of Monuments / legacy recognition
- EverQuest and EverQuest II
- Destiny and Destiny 2
- WoW's decision to keep extending the same persistent world
- Character progress as part of the product
- Social networks and guild history
- Economic resets
- Technology debt versus player attachment
- Whether successful persistent games eventually become impossible to replace

These three work best as progressively broader bridge videos rather than an abrupt channel switch:

1. **Can You Actually Own Something in a Video Game?** — OSRS story, general-gaming conclusion.
2. **Why Are Old Games Winning Again?** — OSRS as one major case among several games.
3. **Why MMO Sequels Almost Never Work** — MMO-wide subject where RuneScape is supporting evidence rather than the main topic.

This progression teaches the audience that The Cube Analyst analyzes game systems, economies and design problems, rather than only Old School RuneScape.
